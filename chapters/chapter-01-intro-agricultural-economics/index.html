Introduction to Agricultural Economics

Study notes · Based on the lecture slides by Dr. Tabassum Zaman

1. Bangladesh’s Agricultural Puzzle

Agriculture generates 10.93% of Bangladesh’s GVA, yet absorbs 44.67% of total national employment.

Core idea: Agriculture contributes a relatively small share of total value added but employs a very large share of workers. The slides describe this as a structural mismatch.

GVA = Gross Value Added.

2. Agricultural Economics: Core Pillars

Microeconomic Duality

In standard microeconomics, consumers and firms are separate. In agricultural economics, a smallholder family is both a producer and a consumer.

  • It chooses how to use inputs such as land, labour and fertilizer.
  • It also decides how much output to consume and how much to sell as marketed surplus.
What does marketed surplus mean?

The part of agricultural output that a farming household sells in the market after meeting its own consumption needs.

3. Land Dynamics & Agro-Ecological Realities

Ricardian Rent & Supply

Land has a fixed biological supply and differs in fertility. These differences create differential economic rent.

In simple terms

Land is not equally fertile or productive. More fertile or advantageous land can generate a higher economic rent.

Von Thünen Spatial Intensity

Distance from urban markets influences crop choice and production intensity:

  • Perishable goods are produced closer to urban markets.
  • Grains can be produced farther away.

Bangladesh context (as stated in the slides)

  • Arable land shrinks by 1% annually due to urban expansion.
  • Cropping intensity reached 179%.
  • Examples of agro-ecological conditions: Barind tube-wells and Haor flash floods.

4. Institutions: Land Tenure & Tenancy

Institutional factors are the formal and informal rules, legal frameworks and organizational networks that govern how farming markets operate. They determine who possesses resources and how transactions are structured.

Two tenancy systems

Sharecropping (Barga)Fixed cash lease (Kuth)
  • Traditional 50–50 output split.
  • Tenant pays 100% of marginal input costs.
  • Tenant receives only 50% of marginal yield.
  • Creates weak incentives to invest in modern technology.
  • Tenant pays a fixed lease fee.
  • Tenant keeps 100% of additional marginal returns.
  • Encourages extra input investment and higher productivity.

Marshallian inefficiency: In sharecropping, the tenant bears all additional input costs but receives only part of the additional output. This discourages investment.

5. Institutions: Credit & Output Markets

Informal credit (Mohajons)Rural moneylenders may charge very high interest because farmers lack formal collateral. Farmers may be forced into distress sales immediately after harvest, when crop prices are low.
Formal interventionsThe slides mention state credit disbursement targets exceeding ৳37,000 crore, increasingly delivered through mobile financial services such as bKash.
Output intermediariesMulti-tier Faria–Bepari supply chains reduce farmers’ margins and often operate without providing local storage infrastructure.
Market solutionsCooperative storage networks and coordinated wholesale-market integration can strengthen farmers’ bargaining power.

6. Government Interventions & Market Stabilizers

The Cobweb Theorem

Agricultural production has a natural time lag. Farmers base the next season’s production on current prices.

  1. High current prices encourage farmers to produce more next season.
  2. The increased supply can cause prices to fall.
  3. Low prices may then reduce production in the following season.

The slides describe this as boom-and-bust price cycles and identify price floors as a possible stabilizing measure.

Public Goods & R&D

Private firms may underinvest in basic crop research because seed genetics are difficult to patent for fragmented smallholders. Public research networks such as BRRI help fill this gap.

Fiscal Safety Cushions

The slides mention more than ৳25,000 crore in targeted fertilizer/diesel subsidies and open procurement to protect rural production and support stable urban manufacturing wages.

7. Structural Transformation

The lecture presents four connected stages:

  1. Physical boundary: Fixed land limits and ecological risks determine basic production capacity.
  2. Institutional rules: Secure leasing arrangements and access to credit allow modern technology to be used.
  3. Stabilizing shocks: Government floor pricing and public R&D help overcome efficiency barriers.
  4. Final takeoff: Marketed crop surplus and released labour support national industrial transformation.
